## Cross-source findings

1. **Specificity is the strongest shared rule.** The employer, role, problem, action, method, and consequence should be identifiable. A sentence that survives replacing the company name is usually too generic.
2. **A letter is an argument, not a CV in paragraphs.** Select one or two requirements, give a concrete event, show the decision/action, and explain what the experience lets the candidate do for this employer.
3. **The human signal is reasoning.** A useful letter contains a real reaction, uncertainty, trade-off, check, disagreement, or decision. These details cannot be recovered from job-title keywords.
4. **The hook must earn its place.** Research can open the letter, but only if it leads to a candidate-specific question or observation. Corporate praise, generic mission alignment, and “I am excited” are weak hooks.
5. **Voice is not artificial imperfection.** The sources support plain, direct, readable prose and human review. They do not support fake typos, slang, awkward fragments, or detector chasing.
6. **AI has a narrow safe role.** It can help extract JD requirements, compare evidence, and correct grammar. It should not invent motivation, outcomes, company events, or personal connections. The candidate must make the final choices and verify facts.
7. **Scoring should have barriers.** A letter should fail regardless of polish if it has no active/verified JD, no company-specific reason, no supported story, an invented fact, or no clear employer benefit. A high keyword score cannot compensate for those failures.
